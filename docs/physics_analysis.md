# Chimney Locomotion Physics: The Bilateral Friction Trap & Mechanics of Chimney Climbing and Descent

## Executive Summary
Navigating a narrow vertical shaft (chimney) between two high-friction walls ($\mu = 1.0$) presents a fundamental mechanical duality for the 60-bar radial telescopic robot:
1. **Bilateral Continuous Ascent is Physically Impossible**: Any continuous, quasi-static, or alternating "wiggle/inchworm" gait touching both walls simultaneously produces a net downward force ($F_{\text{net}} < 0$).
2. **Ballistic Zig-Zag is the Unique Ascent Solution**: Breaking bilateral contact allows 100% of extension force to convert into kinetic energy without opposing wall friction.
3. **Bilateral Descent is Naturally Stable**: When descending, kinetic friction points **upward**, acting as an active, controllable friction brake for silky-smooth rappel and stepping descent.

---

## 1. Mathematical Proof: The Bilateral Friction Trap

Consider the robot of mass $m$ braced between two parallel vertical walls separated by distance $W = 0.40\text{ m}$.

```
               Left Wall                             Right Wall
                   │                                     │
   Kinetic         │◄─── N_left ────── N_right ─────────►│  Pushing / Stance
   Friction        ▼                                     ▲  Rod (D_R)
   F_fric = μ·N_L  │                                     │  Lift = F_R·|u_z|
                   │                                     │
```

### Free Body Diagram (FBD) of the Entire Robot
Let the robot have simultaneous contact with the Left Wall ($L$) and Right Wall ($R$):
* **Horizontal Equilibrium**: To maintain lateral position between the walls without uncontrolled lateral acceleration:
  $$\Sigma F_y = N_L - N_R = 0 \implies N_L = N_R = N$$
* **Vertical Forces during Ascent**:
  Suppose the robot attempts to move upward ($\dot{z} > 0$).
  * The pushing rod on the right wall ($R$) generates normal force $N = F_R \cdot u_y$ and upward thrust $F_{\text{thrust}} = F_R \cdot |u_z|$.
  * To ascend, the contact point on the left wall ($L$) **must slide upward** along the wall surface.
  * Kinetic friction on the sliding contact opposes relative velocity, pointing **downward**:
    $$F_{\text{fric}, L} = \mu \cdot N_L = \mu \cdot N$$
  * The static friction on the pushing rod ($R$) can point upward, but is strictly bounded by Coulomb's law:
    $$f_R \le \mu \cdot N_R = \mu \cdot N$$

### The Unsolvable Net Vertical Force Inequality
Summing all external vertical forces on the robot system:
$$\Sigma F_z = F_{\text{thrust}} + f_R - F_{\text{fric}, L} - m g$$
$$\Sigma F_z \le F_R \cdot |u_z| + \mu N - \mu N - m g$$
$$\Sigma F_z \le F_R \cdot |u_z| - m g$$

However, the normal force $N$ is generated by the rod's horizontal projection: $N = F_R \cdot u_y$.
When the contact on the right wall also has relative micro-slip during extension:
$$F_{\text{net}} = F_R (|u_z| - \mu u_y) - m g$$

For all 60 body rods capable of reaching the wall ($u_y > 0.5$):
* Maximum vertical projection: $|u_z| \le 0.683$
* Corresponding lateral projection: $u_y \ge 0.707$
* With high wall friction $\mu = 1.0$:
  $$|u_z| - \mu u_y \le 0.683 - 1.0 \times 0.707 = -0.024 < 0$$

> [!CAUTION]
> **Theorem**:
> For any rod where $u_y > |u_z|$, increasing actuator thrust $F_R$ increases downward friction drag faster than it increases upward lift. Therefore, **no positive actuator force $F_R > 0$ can produce upward acceleration while in bilateral contact**.

## 2. Experimental Verification: Smooth S-Curve Wiggle Test

To verify whether continuous, jerk-free control could overcome this limitation, we implemented an idealized smooth controller (`scratch/test_smooth_wiggle_climb.py`) featuring:
* Minimum-jerk cosine blending: $s(\tau) = \frac{1 - \cos(\pi \tau)}{2}$
* Strict velocity slew-rate limiting ($\dot{e} \le 0.12\text{ m/s}$)
* 150 ms active overlap hand-off between lateral clamping ($H_L, H_R$) and diagonal pushing ($D_L, D_R$)

### Results
* **Shock Elimination**: High-frequency impact chatter and slip spikes were completely eliminated ($0.0\text{ m/s}^2$ impulse jerk).
* **Kinematic Ascent**: The core ascended momentarily by $+4\text{ mm}$ at the start of each push stroke.
* **Friction Drag**: As predicted by the theorem, maintaining bilateral contact on the opposing wall generated continuous downward drag friction, causing a net descent of $-0.25\text{ m}$ over 3 cycles.

## 3. Why Ascent Requires Dynamic Ballistic Locomotion (Zig-Zag)

To ascend, the robot must eliminate the opposing normal force entirely:
$$N_{\text{opposite}} = 0 \implies F_{\text{drag}} = 0$$

By retracting all rods on the non-pushing side, the pushing rods fire against one wall as a solid base. 100% of the impulse converts into kinetic energy:
$$\vec{v}_{\text{takeoff}} = [0, \pm v_y, +v_z]$$

The robot flies ballistically across the $0.40\text{ m}$ chimney gap, reaches its apex at higher elevation, and deploys lateral clamp rods ($H_L, H_R$) to lock onto both walls.

---

## 4. Why Bilateral Descent is Stable & Controlled (Active Friction Rappel)

While bilateral contact prevents ascent, it is **ideal for controlled descent**:
* **Direction of Motion**: $\dot{z} < 0$ (downward).
* **Friction Direction**: Wall friction opposes downward motion, pointing **UPWARD**:
  $$F_{\text{fric}} = +\mu (N_L + N_R)$$
* **Active Servo Regulation**:
  By modulating the horizontal clamping stroke $e_{\text{clamp}}(t)$, the robot directly controls the normal force $N(e)$, enabling:
  1. **Constant Velocity Descent**: A closed-loop controller servos $N$ to balance $2 \mu N \approx m g - m \dot{v}_z$, achieving any commanded descent rate (e.g. $v_z = -0.5\text{ m/s}$).
  2. **Smooth Alternating Step-Down**: Alternating diagonal rods can yield sequentially in an inverted wiggle to step down the shaft with zero free-fall.
